On Tuesday Pawlikowski came to attend the San Francisco premieres of *Fatherland*, hosted by the film historian and writer David Thomson. I am more than a little embarassed at not knowing who Thomson was, until Pawlikowski thanked him and spoke about how much he had enjoyed reading his books as a film student.

*Faust* is one of those foundational mythologies of European literature. there is goethe, there is marlowe. bulgakov places mephisto in moscow during stalin's great purges, where he fits pretty well, more or less 

so it is easy (perhaps too easy) to see it as Pawlikowski's interpretation of *Faust*.

Thomas Mann travels through Europe

Hard not to contrast with fellow countryman Kieślowski's Blue thirty years later (re-read journal entry from 2023 and see again)

european unification after the cold war as something to be celebrated instead of the post-WWII division of Thomas Mann

once painted in blue, europe is now black and white

forty years passed between thomas mann's trip and the events of blue, and thirty years after that, it is not clear at all

as I am writing these words, there is a constitutional crisis in poland and major ongoing protests in france and germany.

faust is constantly cited, certainly by mann himself. the soviet colonel tulpanov quotes mephistopheles when first meeting mann
(not sure if this is historical)

thomas mann is afraid of being seen as a pawn by either side of the cold war, or lending legitimacy

appealing to a higher spiritual power almost seems quaint given the political situation.







(I am writing this nearly a week after the event, so all speech is paraphrased and should not be taken as a direct quote. I am sure that my own interpretation has unavoidably affected my memory in places.)

Thomson's questions frame a lot of the discussion:
- Q: (in context of ) what is your fatherland?
- A: europe. an ideal of europe, as much as it is now being challenged.


- Q: cinematography. lots of lightness. especially in ida. how did you do that. I see it as a kind of holiness
- A: pawlikowski seemed unsure how to answer the question. (I think from a cinematographic perspective, some amount of contrast may have been edited in post-production).

one of the notable techniques in ida is that they pushed a lot of the action into the very bottom third or quarter of the screen. the effect is to create a sense of distance (like schrader's distancing effect) and to make the characters seem small in the world around them. in pawlikowski's own words, this was completely unintentional. they just started doing it during one of the shots, decided that it worked, and continued doing it.

later some critics interpreted it as the space as the silence of god, or the absence thereof. he seemed a little amused at all of this.

from the perspective of a writer and filmmaker I would also like to confirm that it is difficult to start with a pre-determined symbolism or notion in mind. that is simply not how the creative process works, most of the time, especially for a poetic work.


- Q: pre-occupation with suicide
- A: I don't know how he responded to this

in the film (as in history) Thomas Mann quotes Clemençeau in saying that Goethe did not succumb to the germanic death cult


thomson also said one or two things that I was less certain about, or found myself disagreeing with. "you are the only elliptical filmmaker".
pawlikowski almost seemed embarassed by the question, and said, well it comes naturally to me when writing. (I think the same could be said of any serious student of writing or film, to remove as much as possible.)

this is not to mention the large number of other film directors currently working in the transcendental style, much of which is about withholding.






The audience Q/A was had some interesting questions, as well as some rather poor ones. Another friend confirms that this usually happens at audience Q/As. Not really worth writing about. The questions were quite basic, like "how do you work on a multi-lingual set"? Or very generic, like "what is your creative process for selecting music?"

Pawlikowski seemed tired

- he does not try to make a point. rather he tries to understand the cultural logic of the time

- someone asked him a question about script. I forget the question exactly, but I remember reading something he said years ago, that he constantly rewrites the script, even up until shooting.

importantly, he has a first version of the script that he needs to show the investors. even pawlikowski! one of the more acclaimed art film directors working today! needs to manipulate the script in order to get investment. my first reaction was: how depressing. then I realised that it was also somewhat liberating, to expect disappointment is to not be disappointed. if even pawlikowski has to do this, then I shouldn't be bothered really. if one feels strongly that one's art is morally necessary, then some ends (expression) justify some means (dealing with investors, production companies, executive producers, etc).



Unfortunately the event was not scheduled very well. As a result, I had to catch him in between the premiere at the Metreon and another one at the Kabuki. that's when I asked him about mirror

thanked him for ida, one of the first art films I saw.

also asked him about mirror because he writes (get page link) closest film came to poetry.

so I was curious about his perspective given we both seem to greatly appreciate it.

notably he avoids dreams and stuff like non-diegetic music in his other work. there's a certain emotional rigour. but in *Fatherland*, there is a sequence (erika wandering through the garden, while some score music, modern in its tonal ambiguity, plays). in another scene thomas mann dreams of attending the funeral for his dead son. he is delivering a speech, but when his mouth opens, no words come out. it is a beautiful and frightening vision.

at once his characters are also deeply embedded into history



He was very funny. He said: Are you willing to suffer for years of your life to edit something and make something that might not work? There is also the risk: it is a fine line between making something ambitious and poetic, and an undifferentiated mess. Tarkovsky almost killed himself making Mirror, and he had the whole film industry of the Soviet Union supporting him.
When he was younger he might have been interested, when he could spend so much money without worrying as much (? I'm not sure what he meant by this considering he has many more resources now?), then he might have tried to make it. But if he were to try to make something like Mirror now, he would do it in writing, not film. Even T. S. Eliot couldn't edit The Waste Land, and it was his friend Ezra Pound that had to edit it for him.
