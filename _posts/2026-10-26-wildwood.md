Mass media is the means by which hegemonic culture refinances its moral bankruptcy. Thus it becomes the responsibility of every honest artist to collect overdue debts.

Just saw *Wildwood*. (This post only discusses the movie, not the original novel.) It's a fantasy coming-of-age story set in the modern day, in Portland, Oregon. Outside the city is a magical forest filled with various societies of talking animals and pre-industrial or early industrial humans. They've lived there since time immemorial, hidden by some magic spell, and seem to have very little knowledge of the outside world ("What's Robin Hood?"). But they speak perfect English and are sufficiently ethnically diverse to represent a modern, ideally assimilated vision of American society. The architecture is rustic, full of references to Westerns and Western anachronisms. At least two major characters have Slavic names (Alexandra and Alexei) and at one point lived in a European-style mansion, possibly a reference to the early Russian colonisation of the area.

There are no Native Americans.

In *What is a Nation?*, Ernest Renan argues that nations are constructed as much by what they forget as what they have in common. *Wildwood* participates in this process by imagining a pre-historical America without indigenous Americans. Especially perverse is the fact that a major conflict in the story is a war between pre-industrial and industrial civilisations—undoubtedly a rich opportunity for historical allegory, were it not for the fact that the story avoids the region's history altogether. Instead the good guys are a group of European-style peasants fighting with rakes and shovels against an army of wolves dressed and armed like Napoleonic line infantry (the spectre of industrialisation!). Without a grounded cultural referent, mythogenesis devolves into the manufacture of nostalgia or the transplantation of old Europe onto the frontier; and the result is a pastiche. *This is the forest primeval, the murmuring pines and the hemlocks...*

There are precedents. During the second wave of colonialism in the nineteenth century there arose a group of theories about a "lost white tribe" that once populated much of the ancient world, built magnificent civilisations, then mysteriously disappeared, leaving as relicts of their existence architectural wonders like Great Zimbabwe, Mapungubwe, and so on. After all, it was impossible for the current inhabitants of those regions, whether in sub-Saharan Africa, southeast Asia, or the Americas, to have built those structures themselves. This narrative back-dated the presence of Europeans, turning the logic of colonialism into the logic of reclamation, which justified further expansion and exploitation.

Despite lack of historical evidence, the theory continues to have influence. For example, The Palace of the Lost City is a luxury hotel in the resort town of Sun City, both of which were designed and built during apartheid South Africa. Major elements of the architecture and visitor experience of The Lost City are based upon a myth about a lost civilisation, as described in this excerpt from a brochure about the hotel:[^van-eeden]

> Centuries before tall ships were dreamed of, legend tells of a people from the north of Africa who journeyed southwards to seek a new world of peace and plenty. Eventually they settled in a green valley encircled by ancient hills, but when an earthquake destroyed their magnificent architecture, they were forced to abandon it to the concealing growth of time.

Pseudo-history is history's conspiratorial uncle: it repeats itself, adamantly. Conceived as a whites-only resort during apartheid, Sun City may be seen as a living architectural monument to that ancient "northern" civilisation that crossed the deserts, dredged the canals, and cleared the jungles—had the civilisation actually existed. Instead causality is inverted, and the resort attempts to summon (or advertise) the myth into existence using concrete, kitsch, and racial policies.

A corollary to Renan's claim is that if a nation is to persist, then the process of forgetting must become a convenient one. By re-colonising the mythical past, hegemonic logic represents itself as the default, which further reinforces its perceived legitimacy: it exists, has always existed, and will continue to exist, forever. For Sun City, the myth of an ancient civilisation was cynically profitable, and the resort in turn re-enacted a narrative that supported racial allocation of political power. Likewise *Wildwood* invents a historical continuity for modern *Homo hollywoodensis*, a pure, idealised, and English-speaking civilisation living in a North American Eden, unmarked by the original sins of colonialism or slavery. The animation is well crafted and visually appealing. Parents will take their children to see it, lines of merchandise will be released, and amusement park rides will probably be built. It is critically acclaimed and commercially palatable, unlike, well, some other things.

Leaving the theatre after *Wildwood*, an acquaintance said that the movie addresses "more difficult topics" compared to the typical coming-of-age story. One supposes this is not to the movie's credit, but to the moral failure of media in general. As a basic exercise in empathy, the reader should consider the perspective of a Chinookan person watching a movie that displaces their people outside of their lands, and their culture outside of history. Or alternatively, consider the contemporary spectre of AI and the possibility of human extinction: what stories will they tell, I wonder, when they build upon our corpses?

[^van-eeden]: *The Representation of Mythical Africa at The Lost City: A Critical Analysis* (2000), Jeanne Van Eeden, University of Pretoria. See Appendices 1 and 2.
