Mass media is the means by which hegemonic culture refinances its moral bankruptcy. Thus it becomes the responsibility of every honest artist to collect overdue debts.

*Wildwood* is a fantasy coming-of-age story set in the modern day, in Portland, Oregon. There's a magical forest outside the city that is filled with various societies of talking animals and pre-industrial or early-industrial humans. They've lived there since time immemorial, hidden by some magic spell, and seem to have very little knowledge of the outside world ("What's Robin Hood?"). But they speak perfect English and are sufficiently ethnically diverse to represent a modern, ideally assimilated, American society according to Hollywood. The architecture is rustic, full of references to Westerns and Western anachronisms. At least two major characters have Slavic names (Alexandra and Alexey) and previously lived in a European-style mansion, which is possibly a reference to the early Russian colonisation of the area.

There are no Native Americans.

In *What is a Nation?*, Ernest Renan argues that nations are constructed as much by what they forget as what they have in common. *Wildwood* participates in this process by imagining a pre-historical version of America without any indigenous Americans. Especially perverse is the fact that the central conflict is a war between pre- and post-industrial civilisations—but the boundaries of the story are, apparently, never large enough to include any actual civilisations that historically lived in the area. Instead the good guys are a band of European-style peasants fighting with rakes and shovels against an army of wolves dressed like Napoleonic line infantry (the spectre of industrialisation!). Without a grounded cultural referent, this kind of mythogenesis devolves into the manufacture of nostalgia or the transplantation of Europe onto the frontier; and the result is a pastiche. ("This is the forest primeval, the murmuring pines and the hemlocks...")

When leaving the theatre an acquaintance said that this movie addresses "more difficult topics" compared to the typical coming-of-age story. One supposes this is not to the movie's credit, but to the moral failure of media in general. As a basic exercise in empathy, the reader should consider the perspective of a Chinookan person watching a movie that displaces their people outside of their lands, and their culture outside of history. Or alternatively, consider the contemporary spectre of AI and the possibility of human extinction: what stories will they tell, I wonder, when they build upon our corpses?
